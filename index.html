How this program works

Four retention principles, backed by how memory actually forms — and each one is built into the daily routine, not just named once.

PRINCIPLE 01

Active recall

Pulling a term from memory beats re-reading it. Every Friday you explain a concept back — that retrieval is the rep that sticks.

PRINCIPLE 02

Spaced repetition

Terms fade without revisiting. Each one resurfaces at Week 1, Week 3, and final review — then again during the school year.

PRINCIPLE 03

Interleaving

Quizzes mix new terms with old ones instead of testing topics in isolation. Harder in the moment, far stronger long-term.

PRINCIPLE 04

Dual coding

Plain words + a video + a real application to your portfolio. Three encodings of one idea, so it has three ways back to you.

From the deskThe junior analysts who last aren't the ones who memorize the most — they're the ones who can explain a position to a client who knows nothing. That's why this program ends every week with you teaching, not me. If you can't say it simply, you don't own it yet.

W1JUL 27 Trading Floor BasicsStocks, bonds, markets, indices 0%▾

≈ 2–3 hrs across the week · watch → read → apply → explain back

📖 Understand it — before you watch anything
Toddler version. A stock is a tiny slice of owning a company — own a slice of Intel and you own a sliver of its factories and its profits. A bond is the opposite: you lend a company money and they pay you back with a little extra. The market is a giant flea market where people trade those slices and loans, and an index is just many companies bundled into one number.

Now the real version, because "slice of a company" hides the thing that actually matters for picking stocks. When you own equity, you're a residual claimant: you get whatever's left after everyone else — employees, suppliers, lenders — gets paid. That's why stocks swing more than bonds. If the company does great, all that leftover is yours (unlimited upside). If it fails, the lenders get the scraps and you can get zero. Bonds sit ahead of you in line, so they're steadier but capped.

This one idea — who gets paid first — quietly runs through everything later: why a bond is "safer," why a struggling company's stock can go to zero while the business keeps running, why lenders care about different things than you do.

On your bookYou own INTC, NVDA, GOOGL, URA as equity — residual slices. If Intel's foundry bet pays off, that upside is yours. If it doesn't, bondholders are paid before you see a cent. That asymmetry is exactly why you demand a higher return from stocks than from a Treasury.

W2AUG 03 The Company Report CardStatements → picking stocks from real numbers 0%▾

≈ 3–4 hrs · this is the backbone of stock-picking — go slow

📖 Understand it — reading a company the way an analyst does
Toddler version. Every company has three report cards. One shows money in vs money out this year (income statement). One is a snapshot of everything it owns vs everything it owes (balance sheet). One tracks where the actual cash moved (cash flow statement). The trick nobody tells you: a company can look profitable on paper and still be running out of cash.
Why this is the whole game for picking stocks

You asked to learn how to pick stocks using financial statements — this week is exactly that. The three statements are linked: profit from the income statement flows into equity on the balance sheet and sits at the top of the cash-flow statement. Trace one dollar through all three and you can do most of what a first-year analyst does.

The single most important reflex: profit is an opinion, cash is a fact. Accounting lets a company record a sale before the cash arrives (an accrual). So when net income is rising but cash isn't, something's off — usually customers who haven't paid. That gap is the number-one early warning sign in equity research.

The lines that actually decide a pick

You don't need every line. You need: revenue (is it growing?), gross & operating margin (does it keep money as it grows?), free cash flow = operating cash − capital spending (the real cash you're buying), and debt on the balance sheet (can it survive a bad year?).

Worked — INTC vs NVDA in one glanceNvidia’s gross margin runs far above Intel’s. Why? Nvidia is fabless — it designs chips and rents TSMC’s factories. Intel owns its fabs, so their depreciation lands in cost of goods and crushes the margin. Same “semiconductor” label, two completely different businesses — and you can only see it by reading the statements. That’s the skill.
The catch to rememberIntel’s free cash flow is suppressed right now by fab CapEx. The entire bull case is that today’s CapEx converts into tomorrow’s FCF. That’s a testable claim you read off the cash-flow statement — not a vibe. → Research Desk: the 8-step pick

W3AUG 10 The Trading DeskOrders, ETFs, futures + reading a chart to time an entry 0%▾

≈ 3 hrs · mechanics + your first real technical-analysis lesson

📖 Understand it — fundamentals pick the stock, technicals pick the moment
Toddler version. A market order says “any price, right now.” A limit order says “only my price, I’ll wait.” The DOM is the live stack of everyone’s waiting orders. An ETF is a basket you buy like one stock. A future is a contract to buy or sell later at a price set now — your NQ.
The distinction that makes technical analysis useful

You asked to judge fair price using both technical and fundamental analysis. Here’s how they actually fit together: fundamentals (Weeks 2 & 5) tell you what to buy and roughly what it’s worth. Technicals tell you when to pull the trigger on something you’ve already decided is fairly priced. They’re not rivals — pros let fundamentals pick the stock and technicals pick the entry.

The chart structure worth knowing (for an investor, not a scalper)

Support / resistance — price levels where buyers or sellers have repeatedly stepped in. A fair-valued stock sitting at support is a better entry than the same stock mid-air. Trend — don’t buy a “cheap” stock that’s in a strong downtrend; you’re catching a falling knife. Moving averages — the 200-day is the crude line between a long-term bull and bear regime. Volume — a breakout on heavy volume is real; on thin volume it’s suspect.

You already have the instinctYour DOM/footprint reading — with your inverted red = bid, blue = ask scheme — is technical analysis at the finest timeframe. Institutional accumulation (big size trading at a level with no price progress) is the same signal a chart shows as support. The instinct transfers straight up to timing an investment entry.
The honest limitTechnicals can’t tell you a stock is worth $70 — only that $52 is a level where buyers showed up before. Treat a chart pattern as a reason to own a business you can’t value and you’ll lose money on great-looking setups. Value first, time second. → Research Desk

W4AUG 17 The Macro DeskThe Fed, rates, inflation — and how they set every price 0%▾

≈ 2–3 hrs · the weather system every stock trades inside

📖 Understand it — why macro decides your discount rate
Toddler version. The Federal Reserve is the economy’s thermostat — it raises rates to cool things down, cuts them to warm things up. Inflation is prices rising so your money buys less. The yield curve plots interest rates across time; when it flips upside-down, it’s historically warned of recession.
The link that matters for picking stocks

Macro doesn’t predict individual stocks — it sets the discount rate that everything is priced against. Here’s the chain, and it’s the bridge to your valuation work: the Fed sets the risk-free rate → that feeds the required return on every stock → that’s the rate you discount future cash flows by → which sets fair value. Raise rates, and every future dollar is worth less today, so prices fall. Macro isn’t a separate subject — it’s an input to valuation.

This is why a rate hike hits growth stocks (NVDA) harder than value stocks. Growth stocks are “long-duration” — most of their value sits in far-future cash flows, which get discounted hardest when rates rise. A steady-earnings value name feels it far less.

Reinforcing your earlier workYou’ve already gone deep on the yen carry trade, credit spreads (HYG), the MOVE index, and cross-asset confirmation stacks. W5AUG 24 Valuation + DiversificationFair price, projecting returns, building the book 0%▾

≈ 4 hrs · the payoff week — everything comes together here

📖 Understand it — is the price fair, and what will it return?
Toddler version. Valuation is your best guess at what a company is truly worth, separate from today’s price. A DCF figures that out by adding up all the cash the business will make in the future, translated into today’s dollars. If your worth-estimate is above the price, it might be cheap.
Determining a fair price (fundamental side)

Two tools. Multiples are the fast screen: a P/E of 20 means you pay $20 per $1 of earnings — cheap or rich only relative to peers and its own history. A high P/E isn’t “expensive,” it’s the market pricing in growth; your job is to judge if that growth is real. DCF is the full answer: project free cash flow, discount it at the rate you built from macro (Week 4) and cost of capital, and you get a fair value per share to compare against the price.

Projecting what you’ll get back over a year-count

You asked how to tell how much a stock will give you over a certain number of years. That’s the same time-value machine, run forward. A holding growing at rate r for n years multiplies by (1 + r)ⁿ.

Worked — projecting VOOSay VOO returns ~7%/yr (a common long-run stock estimate). $10,000 in 10 years: 10,000 × 1.07¹⁰ = $19,672 — nearly double. In 20 years: 10,000 × 1.07²⁰ = $38,697. In 30: ≈ $76,000. The extra decades do the heavy lifting — that’s why starting now is the real edge. → Formula Desk: FV / TVM
The honest caveatThat’s an expected average, not a promise — real years swing wildly around it. Never quote a projection as a guarantee. The math shows the shape of compounding, not a certainty.
Diversification — how your picks fit together

Picking good stocks isn’t enough; how they move together is a separate skill. The lever is correlation — ten stocks that all move together is one bet in ten costumes. Three of your four single names (INTC, NVDA, GOOGL) are tech, and two are the same sub-industry (semis). That’s concentration — fine as a deliberate bet, dangerous by accident. URA is your genuine diversifier; VOO is your broad ballast. → Portfolio Desk: your book, PM-style

Mod 1

Introduction to Corporate Finance

No heavy math yet — but two identities here are the foundation everything else stands on. Learn them as sentences.

The Balance-Sheet Identity

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity
Said as"Everything a company owns was paid for either by borrowing or by owners — so owned always equals owed-plus-owners."
Tells youEquity is the plug: it's what's left for owners after debts. Rearranged, Equity = Assets − Liabilities. This is why shareholders are the residual claimants from Week 1.
WorkedA firm owns $500 in assets and owes $300: equity = 500 − 300 = $200. If assets fall to $350, equity is wiped to $50 — the owners absorb the loss first.
Practice: assets $1.2M, equity $700K — what does it owe?
Liabilities = 1,200 − 700 = $500K. The identity always balances by construction.

The Goal of the Financial Manager

Maximize the value of the existing owners' equity
Said as"Every decision is judged by one question: does it make each current share worth more?"
Tells youNot "maximize profit" (which can be gamed short-term) and not "maximize size." Value per share is the yardstick — it's why NPV, not accounting profit, is the decision rule later.
Mod 2

Financial Statements, Taxes & Cash Flow

The bridge from accounting profit to real cash. This module is where "profit ≠ cash" becomes a formula, not a slogan.

Operating Cash Flow (OCF)

OCF = EBIT + Depreciation − Taxes
Said as"Operating profit, add back the non-cash depreciation charge, subtract the cash taxes actually paid."
Tells youThe cash the core business threw off this year. Depreciation is added back because it's an accounting expense, not a cash outflow.
WorkedEBIT $400, depreciation $100, taxes $84: OCF = 400 + 100 − 84 = $416.
Practice: EBIT $600, depreciation $150, taxes $120 — OCF?
600 + 150 − 120 = $630

Cash Flow From Assets (the big one)

CFFA = OCF − Net Capital Spending − Change in NWC
Said as"Operating cash, minus what you spent on long-term assets, minus the cash tied up in day-to-day working capital."
Tells youThe actual free cash generated for investors (debt + equity holders). This is the grown-up version of the "free cash flow" from Week 2 — and the exact thing a DCF discounts.
WorkedOCF $416, capital spending $150, ΔNWC $40: CFFA = 416 − 150 − 40 = $226.
Practice: why subtract the change in net working capital?
Because growth eats cash: you buy inventory and extend customer credit before you collect. That tied-up cash isn't available to investors, so it comes out. Rising NWC is the classic reason a "profitable" firm has no cash.

Marginal vs. Average Tax Rate

Average = Total Tax ÷ Taxable Income  ·  Marginal = rate on the next dollar
Tells youDecisions use the marginal rate — that's what a new project's extra income gets taxed at. The average rate just describes the past.
Practice: which rate matters for "should I take this project?"
The marginal rate — the project adds income at the top, so it's taxed at the rate on the next dollar, not the blended average.
Mod 3

Working With Financial Statements — Ratios

These read the report card from Week 2. Tie each to a real INTC line and it sticks. Grouped the way the textbook groups them.

Liquidity & Solvency

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Tells youCan it cover short-term bills? Above 1 = yes.
Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities
Tells youSame test, but strips inventory (the hardest current asset to turn into cash fast).
Debt-to-Equity = Total Debt ÷ Total Equity
Tells youHow leveraged the company is. Higher = riskier, magnifies both gains and losses.
Practice: current assets $600, inventory $200, current liabilities $250 — quick ratio?
(600 − 200) / 250 = 1.6. It can cover short-term bills 1.6× even without selling any inventory.

Profitability & Market

Profit Margin = Net Income ÷ Sales
Tells youCents of profit kept per dollar of revenue.
ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholder Equity
Tells youProfit squeezed from each dollar owners put in — a quality signal.
ROA = Net Income ÷ Total Assets
Tells youProfit per dollar of everything the firm uses, debt-funded or not.
EPS = Net Income ÷ Shares ·   P/E = Price ÷ EPS
Tells youEPS = profit per share; P/E = dollars paid per dollar earned. High P/E = pricier, or higher growth expected.
WorkedNet income $100, equity $800 → ROE = 12.5%. Price $40, EPS $2 → P/E = 20×.
Practice: net income $250M, sales $2,000M — profit margin?
250 / 2000 = 12.5% — keeps 12.5 cents of every sales dollar.

The DuPont Identity (why ROE moves)

ROE = Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
Said as"Return on equity breaks into three levers: how profitable, how efficiently assets make sales, and how much leverage."
Tells youWhether a high ROE comes from real profitability or just from piling on debt (the equity multiplier). A favorite exam question.
Practice: which lever inflates ROE without making the business better?
The equity multiplier (leverage). More debt raises ROE mechanically but also raises risk — so a high ROE built on leverage isn't the same as one built on margin.
Mod 4

Introduction to Valuation — Time Value of Money

The spine of the whole course. Every valuation formula later is just this idea repeated.

Future Value

FV = PV × (1 + r)n
Said as"Money today, grown forward at rate r for n years."
Tells youWhat a sum you have now will be worth later.
WorkedPut $1,000 in at 6% for 5 years: 1000 × 1.06⁵ = $1,338.23.
Practice: $2,000 at 5% for 3 years — FV?
2000 × 1.05³ = $2,315.25

Present Value

PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n
Said as"Future money, discounted back to what it's worth today."
Tells youWhat a future payoff is worth in today's dollars — the core of every valuation.
Worked$1,338.23 in 5 years at 6%: 1338.23 ÷ 1.06⁵ = $1,000. (Same numbers, run backwards.)
Practice: $5,000 in 4 years, discount 8% — PV?
5000 ÷ 1.08⁴ = $3,675.15

Solving for the Rate or the Time

r = (FV ÷ PV)1/n − 1  ·  n = ln(FV÷PV) ÷ ln(1+r)
Said as"The same FV equation, rearranged to hunt for the missing piece — the return you earned, or how long it takes."
Tells youAny TVM problem has four parts (PV, FV, r, n) — give three, solve the fourth. This is where students freeze; it's just algebra on the one equation.
Worked$1,000 grows to $1,500 in 4 years — what rate? (1500/1000)^(1/4) − 1 = 1.5^0.25 − 1 = 10.67%.
Practice: $2,000 → $4,000, how long at 9%? (Rule of 72 shortcut)
Rule of 72: 72 / 9 ≈ 8 years to double. Exact: ln(2)/ln(1.09) = 8.04 yrs.
Mod 5

Discounted Cash Flow Valuation

Multiple cash flows at once — annuities, perpetuities, and the compounding-frequency traps that eat exam points.

Present Value of a Perpetuity

PV = C ÷ r
Said as"A fixed payment forever, divided by the rate."
Worked$50/year forever at 5%: 50 / 0.05 = $1,000.
Practice: $200/year forever at 8% — PV?
200 / 0.08 = $2,500

Growing Perpetuity

PV = C₁ ÷ ( r − g )
Said as"A payment that grows forever, divided by how far your rate outruns the growth."
Tells youThis IS the Gordon Growth stock model (Module 7) and the DCF terminal value. Same formula, three names. r must exceed g or it breaks.
Worked$50 next year, growing 3% forever, at 8%: 50 / (0.08 − 0.03) = $1,000.
Practice: what happens as g approaches r?
The denominator shrinks toward zero, so PV explodes toward infinity. That's why terminal-growth assumptions above ~4% (long-run GDP) are a red flag.

Present Value of an Annuity

PV = C × [ 1 − (1 + r)−n ] ÷ r
Said as"A fixed payment for a set number of years, all discounted back and added up."
Tells youWhat a loan, a lease, or a stream of bond coupons is worth today. The bracket is the "annuity factor."
Worked$100/yr for 3 yrs at 10%: factor [1 − 1.10⁻³]/0.10 = 2.48685, so 100 × 2.48685 = $248.69.
Practice: $500/yr for 5 years at 6% — PV?
Factor [1 − 1.06⁻⁵]/0.06 = 4.21236 → 500 × 4.21236 = $2,106.18

Effective Annual Rate (EAR) — the compounding trap

EAR = (1 + APR ÷ m)m − 1
Said as"Take the stated annual rate, split it into m compounding chunks, and see what it truly earns once the interest compounds on itself."
Tells youThe real rate once compounding frequency is baked in. A 12% APR compounded monthly is not 12% — it's more. This is the single most common exam trick in this module.
Worked12% APR, compounded monthly (m=12): (1 + 0.12/12)¹² − 1 = 1.01¹² − 1 = 12.68%. The extra 0.68% is compounding.
Practice: 10% APR compounded quarterly — EAR?
(1 + 0.10/4)⁴ − 1 = 1.025⁴ − 1 = 10.38%
Mod 6

Interest Rates & Bond Valuation

A bond is just Module 5 applied — an annuity of coupons plus a lump-sum face value.

Bond Price

Price = (coupons as an annuity) + (face value discounted back)
Said as"Add up today's value of every coupon, plus today's value of the money back at the end."
Tells youWhat a bond is worth when market rates differ from its coupon. Rates up → price down, always.
Worked$1,000 face, 5% coupon ($50/yr), 3 yrs, market rate 6%: coupons 50 × 2.67301 = 133.65 + face 1000 ÷ 1.06³ = 839.62 = $973.27. Below par because 6% > 5%.
Practice: why did that bond trade below $1,000?
The market wants 6% but the bond pays 5%. Price drops until the effective yield equals 6%. Coupon < market rate → discount bond.

Current Yield vs. Yield to Maturity

Current Yield = Annual Coupon ÷ Price
Tells youJust the coupon income relative to price — ignores the gain or loss as price pulls to par.
YTM = the single rate that makes the bond's PV equal its price
Tells youThe all-in return if you hold to maturity — coupons plus the pull to par. It's the bond's version of IRR.
Practice: a discount bond — is its YTM above or below its coupon rate?
Above. You collect the coupon and the price gain as it rises to par, so total return exceeds the coupon rate.

The Fisher Effect (real vs. nominal)

(1 + Nominal) = (1 + Real) × (1 + Inflation)
Said as"The rate you see already has inflation baked in; strip it out to find what you actually gained in buying power."
Tells youApproximately, Real ≈ Nominal − Inflation. Ties straight to your macro Week 4 work on real yields.
Practice: nominal 7%, inflation 3% — approx real rate?
≈ 7% − 3% = 4%. (Exact: 1.07/1.03 − 1 = 3.88%.)
Mod 7

Stock Valuation

A stock is the PV of its future dividends. Three cases, depending on how the dividend grows.

Constant Growth — Gordon Growth Model

P₀ = D₁ ÷ ( r − g )
Said as"Next year's dividend, divided by how much your required return outpaces its growth."
Tells youFair price of a stock whose dividend grows at a steady g forever. Note: it's the growing-perpetuity from Module 5.
WorkedD₁ = $2, r = 10%, g = 4%: 2 / (0.10 − 0.04) = $33.33.
Practice: D₁ = $3, r = 9%, g = 3% — fair price?
3 / (0.09 − 0.03) = $50.00

Zero Growth & the Required-Return Rearrange

Zero growth: P₀ = D ÷ r  ·  Rearranged: r = D₁÷P₀ + g
Said as"A non-growing dividend is a plain perpetuity. And flipping Gordon around, your total return = dividend yield + growth."
Tells youThe rearrange is the exam favorite: r = dividend yield + capital-gains yield (g). It splits a stock's return into income + growth.
Practice: D₁ = $2, price $40, g = 5% — required return?
r = 2/40 + 0.05 = 5% + 5% = 10%. Half from dividend yield, half from growth.

Non-Constant (Two-Stage) Growth

Discount each early dividend by hand, then Gordon the rest as a terminal value
Said as"For a company growing fast then settling down: value the fast years one by one, then treat the steady phase as a growing perpetuity and discount that lump back."
Tells youHow to value real growth stocks (NVDA-type) that can't grow 20% forever. This is the messy multi-step problem — the trap is forgetting to discount the terminal value back to today.
Practice: what's the #1 mistake on two-stage problems?
Computing the terminal value with Gordon at year t, then forgetting it sits at year t — you must discount it back t more years to today. Half the class loses points right there.
Mod 8

Net Present Value & Investment Criteria

"Should we do this project?" — five tools that answer it, NPV being the boss the others are judged against.

Net Present Value (NPV) — the decision rule

NPV = Σ [ CFt ÷ (1 + r)t ] − Initial Cost
Said as"Add up the PV of every future cash flow, then subtract what you paid up front."
Tells youDoes the project create value? NPV > 0 → accept. Overrides every other criterion when they disagree.
WorkedInvest $1,000, get $600/yr for 2 yrs at 10%: 600/1.1 + 600/1.21 − 1000 = 545.45 + 495.87 − 1000 = +$41.32 → accept.
Practice: invest $2,000, get $1,200/yr for 2 yrs at 8% — accept?
1200/1.08 + 1200/1.08² − 2000 = 1111.11 + 1028.81 − 2000 = +$139.92 → accept.

IRR, Payback & Discounted Payback

IRR = the rate r that makes NPV = 0
Tells youThe project's built-in return. Accept if IRR > required rate. Can mislead on weird cash-flow patterns — NPV wins ties.
Payback = years to recover the cost (undiscounted)
Tells youHow fast you're made whole. Simple, but ignores time value and everything after payback.
Discounted Payback = same, but on discounted cash flows
Tells youFixes payback's time-value blindness — but still ignores cash flows after the cutoff.
Practice: $1,000 cost, $500/yr — plain payback period?
1000 / 500 = 2 years. Undiscounted — that's its weakness.

Profitability Index (PI)

PI = PV of Future Cash Flows ÷ Initial Cost
Said as"Bang per buck — how much present value you get for each dollar invested."
Tells youAccept if PI > 1 (same signal as NPV > 0). Useful when you're rationing limited capital across projects.
Practice: PV of inflows $1,150, cost $1,000 — PI, and accept?
1150 / 1000 = 1.15. PI > 1 → accept. You get $1.15 of value per $1 spent.
Mod 9–1

Making Capital Investment Decisions

Getting the project cash flows right before you discount them. The rules here are where most real errors hide.

Project Operating Cash Flow

OCF = (Sales − Costs) × (1 − T) + Depreciation × T
Said as"After-tax operating profit, plus the tax saving the depreciation shields."
Tells youThe yearly cash a project throws off. The second term is the depreciation tax shield — depreciation isn't cash, but it lowers taxes, and that saving IS cash.
WorkedSales−costs $500, depreciation $100, tax 21%: 500 × 0.79 + 100 × 0.21 = 395 + 21 = $416.
Practice: what is the "depreciation tax shield" worth?
Depreciation × tax rate. It's the cash you keep because depreciation reduced your tax bill — real money even though depreciation itself isn't a cash outflow.

Which cash flows count — the three rules

Incremental onlyCount only cash flows that change because of the project. Everything else is irrelevant.
Ignore sunk costsMoney already spent (a past study, prior R&D) is gone — never include it. The classic trap.
Include opportunity costIf the project uses a building you could have rented out, the lost rent is a real cost of the project.
Practice: you spent $50K on research last year. Does it enter the NPV?
No — it's a sunk cost. It's spent whether or not you proceed, so it's irrelevant to the go/no-go decision. Including it is the single most common capital-budgeting mistake.
Mod 9–2

Lessons From Capital Market History

The evidence base: what returns and risk have actually looked like, and how to measure them.

Total Return & the Risk Premium

Total Return = (Dividend + Capital Gain) ÷ Beginning Price
Tells youYour true return is income plus price change — never just the price move.
Risk Premium = Risky Return − Risk-Free Return
Tells youThe extra pay for bearing risk. Historically stocks earned a big premium over T-bills — that's why you hold them.
WorkedBuy at $50, get $2 dividend, sell at $55: (2 + 5)/50 = 14% total return.
Practice: bought $100, $3 dividend, now $94 — total return?
(3 + (94−100))/100 = (3 − 6)/100 = −3%. The dividend softened the price loss but you're still down.

Variance & Standard Deviation (measuring risk)

Variance = average of (each return − mean)2  ·  Std Dev = √Variance
Said as"How far returns typically scatter around their average — squared to punish big misses, then square-rooted back to normal units."
Tells youThe standard measure of total risk (volatility). Bigger std dev = wilder swings. This is the σ under the Sharpe ratio and CAPM.
Practice: why square the deviations first?
So positive and negative misses don't cancel out, and so big deviations count much more than small ones. The square root at the end returns the answer to the same units as the returns.

Efficient Markets (the concept)

Tells youIn an efficient market, prices already reflect available information, so you can't reliably beat it without more risk or better information. It's why the textbook leans toward low-cost index investing — and the honest backdrop to your own stock-picking.
Mod 9–3

Return, Risk & the Security Market Line

Splitting risk into the part you're paid for and the part you're not — and pricing the part you are.

Systematic vs. Unsystematic Risk

SystematicMarket-wide risk (rates, recessions) — you can't diversify it away, so you're paid for bearing it. Measured by beta.
UnsystematicCompany-specific risk (a lawsuit, a bad CEO) — you can diversify it away, so the market pays you nothing for it.
The principleOnly systematic risk earns a return. This is the theoretical backbone of the whole Portfolio Desk — diversification kills the unpaid risk.
Practice: your book is 3 semis + URA. Which risk are you loaded with?
Concentrated unsystematic (industry-specific) risk you could diversify away — plus market-wide systematic risk you can't. URA reduces the first.

Beta & the CAPM / Security Market Line

E(R) = Rf + β × ( Rm − Rf )
Said as"The risk-free rate, plus extra pay for how much market risk (β) you take on."
Tells youThe return investors should demand for a stock's risk. β > 1 = swingier than the market. The SML is just this equation drawn as a line.
WorkedRf 4%, beta 1.3, market premium 6%: 4% + 1.3 × 6% = 11.8%.
Practice: Rf = 3%, β = 0.8, Rm = 9% — expected return?
3% + 0.8 × (9% − 3%) = 3% + 4.8% = 7.8%. Beta below 1 → below the market's return.

Portfolio Return & Beta

Portfolio Return / Beta = weighted average of the pieces
Said as"A portfolio's return and its beta are just each holding's number times its weight, added up."
Tells youHow your whole book behaves. (Note: portfolio variance is NOT a simple weighted average — that's where diversification's benefit hides.)
Practice: 60% in β=1.4, 40% in β=0.9 — portfolio beta?
0.6 × 1.4 + 0.4 × 0.9 = 0.84 + 0.36 = 1.20. The book is 20% swingier than the market.
Mod 9–4

Cost of Capital

Where the discount rate r you've used all course actually comes from — the hurdle every project and valuation must clear.

Cost of Equity — two ways

CAPM: Re = Rf + β(Rm − Rf)  ·  Dividend model: Re = D₁÷P₀ + g
Tells youWhat shareholders demand. Two independent routes to the same number — the exam often asks for both and a comparison.
Practice: D₁ = $2, price $40, growth 6% — cost of equity by the dividend model?
2/40 + 0.06 = 5% + 6% = 11%

WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital

WACC = (E⁄V)·Re + (D⁄V)·Rd·(1 − Tc)
Said as"Blend the cost of equity and the after-tax cost of debt, weighted by how much of each the firm uses."
Tells youThe firm's overall financing cost — the discount rate r for NPV and DCF. Debt is cheaper because interest is tax-deductible (that's the 1 − Tc).
Worked70% equity at 11.8%, 30% debt at 6%, tax 21%: 0.7 × 11.8 + 0.3 × 6 × 0.79 = 8.26 + 1.42 = 9.68%.
Practice: why is the debt piece multiplied by (1 − Tc)?
Interest is tax-deductible, so debt's true cost is lower than its stated rate. At 21% tax, a 6% debt cost is really 6% × 0.79 = 4.74% after tax.
How to drill theseRead a formula's sentence, cover the equation, and rebuild it from the meaning. Do the practice cold before revealing. Then run the recall drill at the top of this desk until you're clearing it without notes — that forced retrieval is what makes them stick for the exam, not re-reading. The equations you can rebuild from meaning are the ones that survive.

The Research Desk

How an analyst or PM actually decides to own a stock — or pass on it. The output of all this work is one thing: a thesis you could defend out loud, plus the honest case against it.

From the deskAmateurs collect reasons to buy. Professionals build the bear case first — the reasons they're wrong — and only buy if the bull case still wins. A thesis without a written "what would make me sell" isn't a thesis; it's a hope.

The process, in order

StepThe question you're answeringWhere to look
1 · ScreenIs this even worth my time?Screeners, news, a sector you know (semis, for you)
2 · The businessHow does it actually make money?10-K "Business" section, investor deck
3 · The moatWhat stops competitors from taking it?Margins over time, market share (your INTC work)
4 · The numbersGrowing? Profitable? Drowning in debt?Revenue, FCF, margins, balance sheet
5 · ValuationIs it cheap or expensive vs. worth?P/E, DCF (your Formula Desk)
6 · The thesisWhy own it? What must go right?3 things that have to be true
7 · The bear caseWhy might I be wrong?Pre-mortem: what breaks it
8 · Size & sell ruleHow much, and when do I exit?Position size + sell triggers, set in advance

The one-page thesis template — worked on INTC

Bull case — what must be true1) The foundry (IDM 2.0) strategy wins real external customers. 2) US-based manufacturing becomes a durable moat as chip supply chains re-shore. 3) Margins recover as new fabs reach scale.
Bear case — what breaks it1) Foundry keeps burning cash without landing marquee clients. 2) A competitor (TSMC) stays generations ahead on process. 3) Fab buildout balloons in cost and drags FCF for years.
Sell ruleDecide now, not in the moment: e.g. "I exit if foundry revenue stalls two years running" or "if the moat thesis is disproven." Written in advance = immune to panic.
Why write the bear case before buying?
Because once you own something, your brain starts defending it — you filter out bad news. Writing the bear case first, while you're still neutral, is the only time you'll do it honestly. It also gives you your sell triggers for free: the thesis is dead when the bear case comes true.

"Should I pick it?" — the honest filter

Pass ifYou can't explain how it makes money in two sentences. If the business is a black box to you, valuation is guesswork.
Pass ifThe whole case rests on one thing going perfectly. Fragile theses break.
Pass ifIt's a great company but a rich price. A wonderful business bought too expensive is still a bad investment. Price and quality are separate questions.
Consider ifStrong business + durable moat + reasonable price + a catalyst you can name + a risk you can live with.

The Portfolio Desk

Picking good stocks isn't enough — how they fit together is a separate skill. This is diversification, read through your actual book.

What diversification actually does

The pointIt kills company-specific risk (one firm blows up) — not market risk (everything falls together). You can diversify away the first, never fully the second.
The leverCorrelation. Holding ten stocks that all move together isn't diversified — it's one bet in ten costumes. Real diversification means holding things driven by different forces.
The only free lunchDiversification is often called the one free lunch in finance: it lowers risk without necessarily lowering expected return. That's why it's the first tool, not the last.

Your book, seen the way a PM would see it

HoldingWhat drives itBucket
INTCSemiconductors, US foundryTech / Semis
NVDAAI chips, data center demandTech / Semis
GOOGLAd revenue, cloud, mega-cap techTech
URAUranium miners, nuclear energyEnergy / Commodity
VOO (Roth)The whole S&P 500Broad core
The readThree of your four single names (INTC, NVDA, GOOGL) are tech, and two are the same sub-industry — semiconductors. They'll tend to rise and fall together. That's concentration: a great sector to be bullish on, but a lot of one bet. URA is your genuine diversifier — nuclear/uranium runs on a different clock than chips. And VOO in your Roth is your broad ballast.
Not a sell callThis isn't "dump your tech." Concentration is fine if it's a deliberate bet you understand. The mistake is being concentrated by accident. Now you're not.

The levers you actually have

SectorSpread across industries that don't share one driver (tech + energy + healthcare + financials…).
Asset classStocks, bonds, real assets — they behave differently in different regimes.
GeographyUS vs. international — one region's bad decade needn't be yours.
Core + satelliteA broad index (your VOO) as the diversified core; individual convictions (INTC, NVDA) as smaller satellites around it. Best of both — market return as a floor, room for your own bets on top.

The honest correction: "minimize risk AND maximize growth"

The catchYou can't fully do both at once — they trade off. Higher expected return comes with higher risk; that link is the deal, not a bug to engineer around.
What you can doMaximize return per unit of risk. Diversification, low fees, and a long horizon get you closest to that frontier. That's the real goal — best return for a risk level you can stomach, not the impossible "all upside, no downside."
Your two real leversTime horizon (decades → you can ride out crashes → more stocks) and risk tolerance (what you can hold without panic-selling). Set those honestly and the allocation follows.

The Retirement & Accounts Desk

Which account, why, and what goes inside it. This is where a few good decisions in your twenties compound into enormous differences later — the highest-leverage section in the whole hub.

Read first — not financial adviceI'm not a licensed financial advisor, and nothing here is a personalized recommendation. This is the framework professionals use, so you can make your own informed call. Anything tied to your specific income and goals is worth confirming with a fee-only advisor or your own analysis.

The account types (2026 figures)

AccountTax deal2026 limitBest for
Roth IRAPay tax now, grows & withdraws tax-free$7,500Low bracket now (students, early career)
Traditional IRADeduct now, pay tax on withdrawal$7,500High bracket now, expect lower later
401(k)Pre-tax now (Roth 401k option too)$24,500Anyone with an employer match

Roth income capIn 2026, direct Roth contributions phase out from $153,000–$168,000 MAGI (single). Well above a student income — you're clear.
Earned-income ruleYou can only contribute up to what you earned. Your TD Bank + internship wages are earned income; they qualify.

Roth vs. Traditional — the one idea that decides it

The whole questionIs your tax rate lower now or lower in retirement? Pay the tax whenever your rate is lower.
Why Roth usually wins for youAs a student with part-time and internship income, your tax bracket is about as low as it will ever be. A Roth locks in today's low rate and lets decades of growth come out completely tax-free. If you'll likely earn far more later (an asset-management career), paying tax now is the bargain. This is the standard reasoning for someone in your spot — and it matches the Roth you already opened at Schwab.
The compounding math, roughly
$7,000 growing at ~7% for 40 years becomes north of $100,000. In a Roth, every dollar of that is yours tax-free. In a traditional account, the withdrawal is taxed at your future (likely higher) rate. Same growth, very different take-home. Time is the ingredient — which is why starting at your age is the real edge.

Choosing ETFs — the criteria, not a tip sheet

Reframe"Minimize risk, maximize growth" isn't achievable as stated (see Portfolio Desk). The closest real version: broad diversification + rock-bottom fees + long horizon. That combination is what the evidence supports.
CriterionWhat to wantWhy
Expense ratioAs low as possible (broad index funds are often ~0.03%)Fees compound against you for decades
DiversificationBroad (whole market) over narrow (one theme)Kills single-company risk
Index vs activeLow-cost index, as the defaultMost active funds trail their index net of fees
OverlapDon't stack funds that hold the same thingsOwning three tech-heavy funds isn't diversifying
Common building blocksBroad US (S&P 500 / total market, e.g. your VOO), international (a total-international fund), and bonds added as your horizon shortens. These are the categories most long-term portfolios are built from — you choose the specific funds.
Watch your overlapYou hold VOO (500 big US companies, already ~30% tech) in the Roth and INTC/NVDA/GOOGL directly. That's tech exposure stacked on tech exposure. Not wrong — but know you're doubling down, not diversifying, when you add another chip name.

Asset location — what belongs in which account

Roth IRAYour highest-growth, long-hold assets. Growth here is never taxed, so it's the most valuable place for things you expect to compound hard over decades.
Taxable brokerageFlexibility and access before 59½. Where your active trading lives — individual stock picks, your NQ futures. Just know the tax: gains held >1 year get the lower long-term rate; under a year is taxed as ordinary income.
The ideaSame investment, different account = different after-tax outcome. Put the tax-inefficient, high-turnover stuff where it does least damage, and the big compounders where growth is shielded.

The priority waterfall (a widely-used ordering)

  1. 401(k) up to any employer match — an instant 100% return; never leave it on the table. (Applies once a job offers one.)
  2. Max the Roth IRA — $7,500 for 2026, in your low-bracket years. This is your sweet spot right now.
  3. Back to the 401(k) up to its higher limit, if you have one.
  4. Taxable brokerage — anything beyond that, plus money you might need before retirement, plus your active trading.
Your version todayNo employer 401(k) as a student, so in practice: fund the Roth first (you've started — VOO is a sound core), and use the taxable brokerage for your individual picks and NQ trading. As internship/career income arrives and a 401(k) match appears, slot it in at the top.
From the deskHere's the part that matters most: the specific fund you pick matters far less than starting early and contributing consistently. A boring low-cost index fund, funded every month for forty years, beats a clever fund picked late almost every time. You already have the account and the core. The whole game now is consistency.
